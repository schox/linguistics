# Knossos

The principal Bronze Age site on Crete, and the source of the tablet corpus that made [Linear B](../Decipherment/linear-b.md) readable.

## Excavation

Minos Kalokairinos, a Cretan antiquarian, dug preliminary trenches in 1878 and 1879. Arthur Evans began large-scale excavation in 1900 for the British School at Athens, working with Duncan Mackenzie and David George Hogarth, and recovered thousands of Linear B tablets.

## Why a site note matters

This is the concrete case for treating provenance as causal rather than incidental. The Linear B tablets from Knossos, later joined by the Pylos material, are why Ventris and Chadwick had enough text to work with, and why the decipherment could be tested rather than merely proposed.

Turn it around and the point sharpens. [Linear A](../Decipherment/linear-a.md) survives in about 1,427 documents carrying some 7,362 to 7,396 signs, against more than 4,600 documents and 57,398 signs for Linear B, on the figures given by [Petrolito et al. 2015](../references/petrolito-et-al-2015.md). That is roughly a third of the documents but only an eighth of the text, and it sits below the threshold at which a proposed reading can be decided. The difference between a solved script and an unsolved one is, to a significant degree, a difference in what happened to be excavated. See [The evidentiary threshold and unicity distance](../Decipherment/evidentiary-threshold-and-unicity-distance.md).

The comparison also needs [Hagia Triada](hagia-triada.md) to be complete. Knossos yielded the Linear B archive; Hagia Triada yielded the largest Linear A archive, and it was still not enough. Provenance is upstream of failure as well as of success.

That makes excavation history a legitimate research topic for this vault rather than background, since the constraint on decipherment is archaeological before it is methodological.
