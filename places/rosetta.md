# Rosetta

A town in the Nile Delta, Rashid in Arabic, and the findspot of the Rosetta Stone. It is the one place in this vault whose importance rests on a mistake about where something came from.

## Findspot is not provenance

The stone was found in July 1799 by Pierre-François Bouchard, a French officer, during work on the defenses of Fort Julien a couple of miles north-east of the town.

It was not made there and had not stood there. Dalby's account: the stele was almost certainly erected in a temple, possibly at the royal town of Sais further inland; the temple was probably closed after 391 when Theodosius I ordered non-Christian temples shut; the stele broke at some point, and the largest surviving piece was reused as building material in a fortress built at Rashid by the Mameluke Sultan Qaitbay, where it lay for at least three centuries.

So Rosetta is where the object was recovered, not where it was inscribed, and the name that attaches to the most famous inscription in the world belongs to a town that had nothing to do with making it.

This is worth a note precisely because the vault's other sites are the opposite case. [Knossos](knossos.md), [Hagia Triada](hagia-triada.md), [Mohenjo-daro](mohenjo-daro.md) and [Enkomi](enkomi.md) matter because material was produced and used there, which is why `found_at` is treated as evidence about a corpus. Rosetta is the reminder that a findspot can be an accident of later reuse, and that reading provenance off a findspot is a mistake the vault should not make elsewhere.

## What happened to the stone

Recognized immediately as significant, reported to Napoleon's Institut d'Égypte, and copied by lithography and by using the inked stone itself as a printing block, so that European scholars had the texts before anyone had the object. The stone passed to British possession under the Capitulation of Alexandria in 1801, reached Portsmouth in February 1802, and has been in the British Museum almost continuously since June 1802 as EA24. Dalby records that it is the museum's most-visited single object.

The copies matter more than the custody. Because prints circulated from 1800 to 1802, the decipherment was carried out across several countries by people who had mostly never seen the stone.

## Open questions

- **The stone is not the only witness.** Three further fragmentary copies of the Memphis decree are known, along with the Nubayrah Stele and an inscription on the Philae obelisk. The Rosetta Stone is no longer unique, and Dalby says so plainly. The vault has no note on the decree itself, which is arguably the better subject than the object.
- **No `pleiades` identifier looked up.** Rosetta is a medieval and modern town rather than a classical site, so it may fall outside the gazetteer's scope, but this has not been checked. `geonames` would be the more appropriate join and is also unfilled.
- **Sais has no note**, despite being the likely original home of the stele. Whether it earns one under the inclusion test depends on whether the attribution is more than a probability.
- **The British Museum's own record was unreachable**, returning 403, so everything here about the object comes from Dalby rather than from the holding institution.
- **Repatriation is not covered.** Dalby notes standing demands for the stone's return to Egypt. That is a live question about a contested object and the vault should either treat it properly or not gesture at it.
