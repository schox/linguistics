# Harappa

A Bronze Age urban site in Punjab, Pakistan, and the type-site of the civilization named after it. One of the two principal provenances of the [Indus script](../Decipherment/indus-script.md) corpus.

Yadav and colleagues state the naming convention plainly: the remains of the civilization were first found at Harappa, and by historical convention the civilization is called Harappan after it. The terms Harappan and Indus are used interchangeably in the literature, including for the script.

## Excavation and extent

Excavation began in 1921, a year before [Mohenjo-daro](mohenjo-daro.md) was identified. UNESCO's tentative-list documentation describes a series of low mounds and cemeteries south of a dry course of the Ravi river, with a full extent of 150 hectares, of which the property and buffer zone cover eight mounds and two cemeteries; the rest lies under farmland and the modern village. The archaeological sequence is over 13 metres deep and runs from the fourth to the second millennium BCE, and the site reached its full extent of over 100 hectares during the mature Harappan period.

The excavators' chronology, as UNESCO gives it, is the most precise phase sequence the vault holds for the Indus material:

| Phase | Dates |
| --- | --- |
| Ravi aspect of the Hakra phase | c. 3300-2800 BCE |
| Kot Dijian (Early Harappan) | c. 2800-2600 BCE |
| Harappan | c. 2600-1900 BCE |
| Transitional | c. 1900-1800 BCE |
| Late Harappan | c. 1800-1300 BCE |

**Who began the excavation is not settled by the sources read here.** Britannica credits Sir John Hubert Marshall with initiating and directing the original excavations from 1921. Widely repeated accounts instead credit Daya Ram Sahni, an officer of the Archaeological Survey of India, with beginning work that year under Marshall's overall direction as Director-General. Both cannot be the whole story, and the pattern (a British director-general remembered, an Indian field officer less so) is the kind the vault's rule on credit exists to catch. It is recorded here as unresolved rather than decided, because no source that would settle it has been read.

## Why the site matters here

Harappa is where the material that started the entire problem came from. Farmer, Sproat and Witzel date the beginning of the Indus-script question to the discovery of the first Harappan seal in 1872-3, and note that the assumption the inscriptions encoded language has been near-universal ever since.

The site is also where a specific evidential argument bites. They report large numbers of small inscribed steatite and faience objects at Harappa whose ritual or administrative use remains uncertain, and use the character of what survives, rather than merely its quantity, to argue against a literate reading. Provenance here is not just where the corpus came from; the kind of object it came on is part of the dispute.

## Open questions

- **Who began the 1921 excavation**, as above. Vats's excavation report and the Archaeological Survey of India's own record would settle it. This is worth resolving rather than leaving, because it is a credit question and the vault has a stated rule about those.
- **No per-site corpus count.** How many of the c. 4,000-5,000 inscribed objects come from Harappa is not stated in any source read. [Mahadevan 1977](../references/mahadevan-1977.md) is reported to tabulate sign distribution by site, which would answer it.
- **`pleiades` is empty by design, not omission.** Pleiades is a gazetteer of the Greek and Roman world and is unlikely to cover an Indus site. A South Asian archaeological gazetteer with stable identifiers would be the right join here, and none was identified. `geonames` is also unfilled.
