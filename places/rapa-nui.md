# Rapa Nui

A remote island in the south-eastern Pacific, a special territory of Chile, and the sole provenance of [rongorongo](../Decipherment/rongorongo.md).

**On the name.** The vault uses *Rapa Nui*, the name the island's people use for themselves, their language and their island. *Easter Island* is the common English exonym and is the label Wikidata carries under Q14452; Berthin uses both. This is a departure from the vault's usual practice of following the Wikidata label, made deliberately: for Hagia Triada the variants were competing transliterations of one name, whereas here one name is the community's own and the other commemorates the day a Dutch expedition arrived.

## Why the place matters here

Every other site in this vault matters for what excavation produced. Rapa Nui matters for what happened to the people.

Berthin, citing Fischer, records that most rongorongo scribes and cantors were kidnapped or died in the genocide and disease that accompanied colonization, and that by June 1869, when Bishop Tepano Jaussen brought the script to outside attention, few people able to interpret it are presumed to have remained alive.

That is a different category of loss from anything else in the Decipherment area. [Knossos](knossos.md) and [Hagia Triada](hagia-triada.md) concern what survived three thousand years of accident. [Mohenjo-daro](mohenjo-daro.md) concerns what a century of excavation did not find. Rapa Nui concerns a reading community destroyed within the span of the written record, by identifiable people, while outside observers were present and taking notes.

The vault's framing elsewhere, that decipherment faces obscurity which is "an accident of cultural loss", does not fit this case and should not be stretched to cover it. See [adversarial versus accidental encipherment](../Decipherment/adversarial-versus-accidental.md), which is the note that needs amending, not this one.

## A second consequence: the corpus has no stable boundary

Objects bearing rongorongo-like glyphs have been made for sale to visitors since the nineteenth century and still are. That is a direct result of the island's position in a tourist economy, and it means provenance research on Rapa Nui material is not a formality but part of establishing what the corpus even is. Berthin's paper is an authenticity study for exactly this reason.

## Open questions

- **The settlement date is contested and the note does not resolve it.** `flourished` records c. 1200 CE from general reference rather than from a source read here, and estimates for Polynesian settlement of the island have moved considerably. It bears directly on whether rongorongo could have developed before European contact.
- **No `pleiades` identifier, and none is expected.** Pleiades covers the Greek and Roman world. A Pacific gazetteer with stable identifiers would be the right join and none was identified. `geonames` is unfilled.
- **The excavation and ethnographic record is not covered.** Jaussen, Métraux and the missionary sources are where the nineteenth-century testimony about rongorongo comes from, and none has been consulted directly.
- **Rapanui the language has no note.** It should have one in `Human-Languages/`, on its own merits and as the vault's first Austronesian entry, not as an appendage to a script.
