# Behistun

A trilingual inscription and relief of Darius I, cut high into a cliff face in western Iran, and the long text that made the [cuneiform](../Decipherment/cuneiform.md) decipherment possible.

## The site that had to be climbed

Every other provenance in this vault concerns whether enough text survived. Behistun concerns whether anyone could get at it.

The inscription was intact, long, and trilingual, and it was on a mountainside. Torres Torres describes what Rawlinson did from 1835: hard and dangerous work, lowering himself down the vertical rock wall and staying there for hours while he laboriously copied.

That is a distinct constraint and the vault should name it. A corpus can be lost, it can be too small, it can be unreadable, and it can be **physically inaccessible**. Only the first three appear anywhere else in these notes. Behistun was none of the first three and still waited until someone was willing to hang off it.

## What made it work where Persepolis did not

Rawlinson had already reached results comparable to Grotefend's from trilingual inscriptions at Mount Elwend, near Hamadan, ancient Ecbatana: twenty lines in Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian, on two rocks about two metres high.

Those were short. Behistun is long, and length is what a decipherment needs once the principle is established. This is the same relationship the vault records for the [Phaistos Disc](../Decipherment/phaistos-disc.md), running the other way: 241 signs cannot settle anything, and a long text can.

## Compared with Rosetta

The two great multilinguals of decipherment differ in almost every practical respect, and the comparison is instructive.

[Rosetta](rosetta.md) was portable, was seized as war booty, reached a museum within three years, and was reproduced in prints and casts so that scholars across Europe worked from copies. Behistun stayed exactly where Darius put it, and the copies existed only because one man went up the cliff with a notebook.

The consequence shows in the shape of the two decipherments. [Egyptian](../Decipherment/egyptian-hieroglyphs.md) was worked on by a scattered community with equal access to the evidence, and produced a bitter priority dispute. Cuneiform was worked on by people substantially dependent on Rawlinson's copies, and ended in a coordinated public test rather than a quarrel. That is a suggestion rather than a finding, and the vault records it as such.

## Open questions

- **The physical description is thin.** Height above the ground, dimensions, the number of lines in each version and the content of the relief are not stated here, because the sources read cover the decipherment rather than the monument.
- **The three languages are Old Persian, Elamite and Babylonian**, and the vault has a note for none of them as such. [Akkadian](../Human-Languages/akkadian.md) covers Babylonian only loosely.
- **`pleiades` not looked up.** Behistun is an Achaemenid site and may well have a record, unlike the Bronze Age sites where the gazetteer's scope was the obstacle.
- **Rawlinson's own publication has not been read.** *The Persian Cuneiform Inscription at Behistun* (1846-1851) is the primary record of the copying and is out of copyright.
- **Later copying campaigns are not covered**, including the twentieth-century work that corrected and completed Rawlinson's readings.
