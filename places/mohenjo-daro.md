# Mohenjo-daro

A Bronze Age urban site in Sindh, Pakistan, and with [Harappa](harappa.md) one of the two principal provenances of the [Indus script](../Decipherment/indus-script.md) corpus. The largest city of the Indus civilization, and generally taken to be a capital.

## Excavation

R. D. Banerji, an officer of the Archaeological Survey of India, identified the site in 1922, two years after major excavation had begun at Harappa. Large-scale work followed through the 1930s under John Marshall, K. N. Dikshit, Ernest Mackay and others. The last major excavation was directed by G. F. Dales in 1964-65, after which excavation was banned because exposed structures could not be protected from weathering; since then only salvage work, surface survey and conservation have been permitted. German and Italian teams under Michael Jansen and Maurizio Tosi carried out architectural documentation and surface survey in the 1980s.

Britannica dates the recognition of the site's archaeological importance to 1922 as well, and credits the subsequent excavations to Marshall's program.

**How much has been dug is the number that matters here.** UNESCO records the property as burnt brick structures covering 240 hectares, "of which only about one third has been excavated since 1922".

## Why the site matters here

The argument this site supports is an argument from absence, which makes it unusual among the vault's places.

Farmer, Sproat and Witzel's case that the Indus corpus is not writing rests substantially on what excavation has *not* produced. They observe that every premodern civilization known to have written extensively on perishable materials has left durable archaeological markers of that practice: long texts on pots and stelae, inkpots, styli, palettes, depictions of scribes. Their claim is that Indus sites, excavated on a very large scale over more than a century, have produced none of these, and that the absence is therefore evidence rather than an accident of preservation.

Mohenjo-daro is the largest and most intensively excavated of those sites, which is what gives the negative result its force. A thinly dug site proves nothing by silence. This is the mirror image of the argument [Knossos](knossos.md) supports for [Linear B](../Decipherment/linear-b.md), where what was excavated made a decipherment possible: here what was excavated, and what it failed to contain, is used to argue the problem is not well posed.

The counter-position, in [Rao et al. 2010](../references/rao-2010.md), is that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and that long texts may have been written on materials that did not survive. Both sides are arguing about the same excavations.

**The one-third figure cuts both ways, and the vault should say so.** Farmer, Sproat and Witzel's argument needs the excavation to have been thorough enough that absence is informative; two thirds of a 240-hectare site remaining undug is a real qualification, and one they do not have to concede for their argument to work, since their claim concerns the total absence of a class of find across all Indus sites rather than exhaustive coverage of one. But it is the correct number to hold in mind, and no source read here quantifies the excavated fraction across the corpus of sites as a whole.

## Open questions

- **No per-site corpus count**, as for Harappa. [Mahadevan 1977](../references/mahadevan-1977.md) reportedly tabulates sign distribution by site, and that would let the provenance argument be made in numbers.
- **The excavated fraction across all Indus sites is unknown.** The one-third figure above is for this site alone. Farmer, Sproat and Witzel's argument is about the whole corpus of excavated sites, so the aggregate is the figure their case really depends on, and no source read here gives it.
- **The excavation reports have not been read.** Marshall 1931 and Mackay 1938, both cited by Farmer, Sproat and Witzel, are the primary record. The account above comes from a tertiary encyclopedia and a curated website, which is adequate for an outline and not for detail.
- **`pleiades` and `geonames` are empty.** See the note on [Harappa](harappa.md); Pleiades covers the Greek and Roman world.
