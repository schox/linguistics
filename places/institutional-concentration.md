# Institutional concentration

The observation that particular institutions, for particular periods, produce output wildly out of proportion to their size, and that this is usually structural rather than a run of individual genius.

## The pattern

The clearest illustration comes from outside this vault's subject matter entirely. The University of Padua dominated European anatomy from roughly the fifteenth to the eighteenth centuries. Vesalius held the chair of surgery and anatomy and published *De Humani Corporis Fabrica* there in 1543; Falloppio taught there; William Harvey studied there before describing the circulation; Galileo held the mathematics chair from 1592 to 1610. Its anatomical theatre, built in 1595, is the oldest surviving permanent example in Europe.

What produced that was not a coincidence of talented individuals but a set of conditions: institutional permission to dissect, a physical venue built for the purpose, a teaching lineage in which each generation trained the next, and enough international reputation to attract students who then carried the method elsewhere. Padua does not have a note of its own in this vault, because it fails the intake rules, but it names the phenomenon better than any in-scope example.

## Instances in scope

- **[Bletchley Park](bletchley-park.md)**, 1939 to 1945. Deliberate interdisciplinary recruitment (mathematicians, linguists, classicists, chess players, crossword solvers) under wartime urgency, producing both the industrialization of cryptanalysis and, in Colossus, the first programmable digital electronic computer.
- **[Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, Leipzig](max-planck-institute-leipzig.md)**, 1997 to present. A sustained program rather than a single breakthrough, producing shared infrastructure (Glottolog, WALS, CLDF) that other people's work now depends on.
- **MIT**, for the Chomskyan program in linguistics and, separately and much later, the neural decipherment work of Barzilay and colleagues. Two unrelated concentrations in the same institution, which is itself worth examining.

## Why it is worth a note

Three reasons, in ascending order of interest.

It explains clustering that otherwise looks like coincidence. It suggests that the transferable thing is the conditions rather than the people, which is a testable claim. And most usefully for this vault, it predicts where to look: if method transfer between cryptanalysis, linguistics and computation happens through institutions rather than through literature, then the history of those institutions is evidence about the history of the methods.

## Open questions

- Does the pattern hold for decipherment specifically, or were the major decipherments unusually the work of isolated individuals (Champollion, Ventris, Knorozov, none of them working from a dominant institutional base)?
- If decipherment really is the outlier, that is interesting in itself and cuts against the argument above.
