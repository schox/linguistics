# Frost 2005

A handbook chapter on how the structure of an orthography shapes skilled reading, by one of the two authors of the paper that named the orthographic depth hypothesis. The most authoritative statement of that hypothesis reachable without a paywall.

## What it establishes

- **The definition of orthographic depth** (p. 278). "An orthography that represents its phonology unequivocally following simple grapheme-phoneme correspondences is considered *shallow*, while an orthography in which the relation of orthography to phonology is more opaque is labeled *deep*." Depth "is often regarded as a continuum".
- **The term predates the hypothesis.** Frost attributes "orthographic depth" to Klima 1972, Liberman, Liberman, Mattingly and Shankweiler 1980, Lukatela, Popadic, Ognjenovic and Turvey 1980, and Katz and Feldman 1981, all earlier than the 1992 chapter that named the hypothesis.
- **Two sources of opacity**: phonological, where "the spoken language has assimilated novel phonetic variations during its history, whereas the letters in the orthographic system have remained unchanged"; and morphological, where the orthography is "designed to preserve and convey the morphologic information", as in *heal* and *health*.
- **Depth is not one thing.** English depth comes from irregular and inconsistent letter clusters; Hebrew depth comes from *missing* phonemic information, chiefly vowels. "English and Hebrew are therefore considered deep orthographies, but as shown above, their depth derives from different sources" (p. 280).
- **Opacity has a direction** (p. 280). Frost argues depth "cannot be adequately described through the one-dimensional factor of labeling languages simply as 'deep' or 'shallow'", and that the mapping from orthography to phonology may be transparent while the reverse is opaque. French is his case: print specifies sound almost unequivocally, but *o*, *au* and *eau* all spell /o/.
- **Regularity against consistency** (p. 278), two distinct factors within English. Regularity is conformity to grapheme-phoneme rules, so *yacht* and *chef* are irregular. Consistency is uniqueness of pronunciation for an orthographic body, so MOTH against BOTH makes OTH inconsistent.
- **Strong and weak forms of the hypothesis** (pp. 281-282). The strong form holds that in shallow orthographies phonology is derived exclusively prelexically; Frost states flatly that it "is untenable", because even Spanish, Italian and Serbo-Croatian fail to represent stress. The weak form, which he says gained increasing support, makes the difference between deep and shallow "mainly quantitative, not qualitative".

## Access

Free from a university course site as a scanned offprint of the published chapter. The first page carries the stamp "2005. In M. J. Snowling & C. Hulme (Eds.), The science of reading: a handbook (272-295). Oxford: Blackwell", which is where the bibliographic details above come from.

Read to p. 282 of 272-295, covering the writing-system overview, the language cases and the statement of the hypothesis. The empirical-evidence sections that follow have not been read.

**Katz and Frost 1992 has not been read.** Frost cites it as the discussion of the strong form ("see Katz & Frost, 1992"). Its details, as given by citation aggregators rather than seen: Katz, L. and Frost, R., "The Reading Process is Different for Different Orthographies: The Orthographic Depth Hypothesis", in Frost and Katz (eds), *Orthography, Phonology, Morphology, and Meaning*, Advances in Psychology 94, 67-84, Elsevier. Treat those details as unverified.
