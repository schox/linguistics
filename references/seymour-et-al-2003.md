# Seymour et al. 2003

The cross-linguistic study of first-year reading acquisition across thirteen European orthographies, and the standard empirical citation for the claim that orthographic depth has measurable consequences for learning to read.

## What it establishes

Taken from the published abstract, which is the only part read:

- **Scope.** Letter knowledge, familiar word reading and simple nonword reading, assessed "in English and 12 other orthographies".
- **The general result.** "Children from a majority of European countries become accurate and fluent in foundation level reading before the end of the first school year."
- **The exceptions.** "There are some exceptions, notably in French, Portuguese, Danish, and, particularly, in English."
- **The size of the English effect.** "The rate of development in English is more than twice as slow as in the shallow orthographies."
- **Two separate causes, with different reach.** "Syllabic complexity selectively affects decoding, whereas orthographic depth affects both word reading and nonword reading." Depth is therefore not the only variable, and the study separates it from syllable structure rather than conflating the two.
- **The proposed mechanism**, stated as a hypothesis by the authors: "the deeper orthographies induce the implementation of a dual (logographic + alphabetic) foundation which takes more than twice as long to establish as the single foundation required for the learning of a shallow orthography."
- The abstract records that the effects "appear not to be attributable to differences in age of starting or letter knowledge", which rules out the two most obvious confounds.

## Access

Paywalled at Wiley. The bibliographic details and the abstract above were taken from the National Library of Medicine record, PMID 12803812, which is authoritative for both.

**Only the abstract has been read**, so `status` is `unread` rather than `in-progress`, and nothing above goes beyond what the authors state there. The thirteen orthographies are not named in the abstract, and the per-language figures are not given; both would need the article.

**A second-hand summary of this study is in circulation and is narrower than the abstract.** [Baroni 2011](baroni-2011.md), p. 141, reports it as showing that "Danish and English children lag far behind other children", omitting French and Portuguese. The abstract names four exceptions. This is a small case of the general reason for going to the source rather than to a work citing it.
