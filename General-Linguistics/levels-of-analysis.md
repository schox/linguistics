# Levels of analysis

The standard division of linguistic structure into levels, each with its own units, its own kind of rule, and its own methods. It is the organizing scheme of almost every introductory treatment of the subject, including [Anderson et al. 2022](../references/anderson-et-al-2022.md), from which this subtree is written.

The levels are conventionally ordered from the smallest unit upward.

| Level | Unit | What it studies |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Phonetics](phonetics.md) | phone | the physical signal: how it is produced, transmitted and perceived |
| [Phonology](phonology.md) | phoneme | which combinations of units a language permits, and how they change in combination |
| [Morphology](morphology.md) | morpheme | how words are built |
| [Syntax](syntax.md) | constituent | how words combine into phrases and sentences |
| [Semantics](semantics.md) | denotation | what expressions mean, independent of context |
| [Pragmatics](pragmatics.md) | utterance | what a speaker means by an expression in a context |
| Discourse | text | structure above the sentence: cohesion, topic, turn-taking |

**Discourse has no note yet.** It is named in this area's `_index.md` as part of this subfield, and the source used for the rest of the subtree does not treat it as a level in its own right.

## The boundaries are not sharp

Worth recording, because the table above makes the divisions look cleaner than they are.

- **Morphology and syntax** both concern how meaningful units combine, and differ mainly in whether the combination happens inside or outside a word. That distinction depends on a definition of "word", which [Anderson et al.](../references/anderson-et-al-2022.md) describe as a hotly debated topic, distinguishing phonological, morphological and syntactic words that do not always coincide.
- **Semantics and pragmatics** divide meaning into what a sentence means and what a speaker means by it. The diagnostic is cancellability, but the boundary is a live question in the field and different treatments draw it in different places.
- **Phonetics and phonology** divide the physical from the systematic. The same data serves both.

## Levels are not specific to speech

The levels are usually taught with spoken examples, but the categories are not about sound. Anderson et al. treat signed languages within the phonetics and phonology chapters rather than in an appendix: signed languages have articulators, contrastive units, minimal pairs and combinatory rules, and are analyzed with the same apparatus.
