# Semantics

The study of **linguistic meaning**: what a language user has to know in order to produce and understand meaning. [Anderson et al. 2022](../references/anderson-et-al-2022.md) treat semantics as the meaning an expression has on its own, leaving meaning in context to [pragmatics](pragmatics.md).

## What is excluded

The word "meaning" covers several things, and Anderson et al. delimit the subject by elimination. Their examples:

| Use | Why it is not linguistic meaning |
| --- | --- |
| A tea stalk floating upright in green tea "means" good luck | the stalk is not a linguistic expression |
| There is no *meaning* in buying a sewing machine you cannot use | this is the purpose of an action |
| I said it but I didn't *mean* it | this is sincerity, and belongs to pragmatics |
| *ode'imin* and *strawberry* mean the same thing | **this is the target sense** |

The last is the subject. The Ojibwe (Anishinaabemowin) word *ode'imin* and the English word *strawberry* each carry content that lets a user of that language decide, for any given object, whether the word applies to it. That shared content is what it is for them to mean the same thing.

## Method

A word's meaning cannot be studied in isolation. Anderson et al. state the principle directly: looking at *sigh* by itself is not insightful, and what is needed is how the word is and is not used in sentences of the language. **Sentence meaning is the evidence for word meaning.**

## Entailment

*p* **entails** *q* when *p* being true makes *q* necessarily true.

*The customer sighed* entails *The customer emitted a breath*.

The diagnostic is the **contradiction test**. Negate the candidate with "it is not the case that" and conjoin it to the original with "but":

> ⊥ The customer sighed, but it is not the case that the customer emitted a breath.

A **contradiction** is a sentence that is never true, marked `⊥`. Negating an entailment produces one, and that is what shows the entailment holds. It is impossible to sigh without emitting a breath, and the test makes that requirement explicit.

## Implicature, and how it differs

Some conclusions a hearer draws are not entailments.

*The customer sighed* suggests *The customer is angry*. Applying the test:

> The customer sighed, but it is not the case that the customer is angry.

This is not a contradiction, so anger is not entailed. It is an **implicature**: a possible but not guaranteed conclusion. Formally, *p* implies *q* if, in context, *p* suggests *q* is true without making it necessarily true.

**Cancellability is the diagnostic.** An implicature can be negated without contradiction, because it need not hold; the customer may have sighed with relief. Implicature is developed further under [pragmatics](pragmatics.md).

## Open questions

- **Formal semantics is not covered here.** Denotation, truth conditions and set theory are all in the source and unused, and this area's index names formal semantics separately under **Formal foundations**. The Montague tradition is absent from the vault entirely.
- **Lexical semantics is missing**: sense relations, polysemy, and how a dictionary definition differs from a denotation. The source has a section on why a dictionary is not a semantic theory.
- **Semantic change is not addressed**, which is the historical half of the subject and belongs under **Historical and comparative**.
- **The boundary with pragmatics is stated and not examined.** Where different treatments draw it, and on what grounds, is a live question the source does not survey.
