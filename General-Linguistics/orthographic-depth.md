# Orthographic depth

How directly an alphabetic orthography maps its written signs onto the sounds of the language. An orthography that "represents its phonology unequivocally following simple grapheme-phoneme correspondences is considered *shallow*, while an orthography in which the relation of orthography to phonology is more opaque is labeled *deep*" ([Frost 2005](../references/frost-2005.md), p. 278).

Depth is a property of an orthography, not of a script or of a writing system, and the three are distinguished in [Script typology](script-typology.md). English and Italian use the same script and the same writing system, and differ almost entirely in depth. The mapping at issue is to [phonology](phonology.md), the system of contrasts, rather than to the phonetic signal.

The term is older than the hypothesis built on it. Frost attributes it to work by Klima in 1972 and by Liberman and colleagues, Lukatela and colleagues, and Katz and Feldman around 1980, all predating the 1992 chapter that named the orthographic depth hypothesis.

## Depth is a continuum, not a pair of boxes

Frost records that depth "is often regarded as a continuum", along which languages can be ordered, "where one language would be considered deeper than another but shallower than a third one".

The usual anchors at the shallow end are Finnish, Serbo-Croatian, Spanish and Italian, and at the deep end English, French, Danish and unpointed Hebrew. [Baroni 2011](../references/baroni-2011.md) calls Finnish "the most common example of a shallow orthography", with each grapheme corresponding to one phoneme, the digraph for the velar nasal being the sole exception, and vowel and consonant length both written by doubling (p. 143).

Even the anchors need qualifying. Baroni notes that Italian, usually treated as transparent, "is less shallow than others": it keeps etymological letters, a silent *i* in *scienza*, and an *h* that marks forms of *avere* without corresponding to any sound (p. 144).

## Two things called depth

The single label covers at least two distinct situations, which is the sharpest point in Frost's treatment.

**Inconsistency.** English writes its roughly fifteen vowels with fewer graphemes, and the correspondences that result are neither regular nor consistent. Frost separates those two: **regularity** is conformity to grapheme-phoneme rules, so *yacht* and *chef* are irregular; **consistency** is uniqueness of pronunciation for an orthographic body, so MOTH beside BOTH makes OTH inconsistent (p. 278).

**Missing information.** Hebrew depth is of another kind entirely. Vowels are mostly optional diacritics, absent from ordinary text, so "a printed consonant string is always phonologically ambiguous and often represents more than one word, each with a different meaning". Pointed Hebrew, by contrast, "is entirely shallow" (p. 279).

Frost's conclusion is that "English and Hebrew are therefore considered deep orthographies, but as shown above, their depth derives from different sources" (p. 280). The second kind is a property of the writing system's category rather than of its spelling conventions, and it is what an abjad is: see [Script typology](script-typology.md).

## Opacity has a direction

A further reason a single scale is insufficient. Frost argues that depth "cannot be adequately described through the one-dimensional factor of labeling languages simply as 'deep' or 'shallow'", and that the direction of the mapping matters: reading and spelling are not the same problem (p. 280).

- **French** is transparent from print to sound and opaque from sound to print: the spelling specifies the pronunciation almost unequivocally, but *o*, *au* and *eau* all write /o/.
- **English** is opaque in both directions.
- **Spanish, Italian and Serbo-Croatian** are transparent in both.

Frost reports that the distinction has measurable effects on word recognition, labeled feed-forward against feed-backward consistency by Stone, Vanhoy and Van Orden in 1999.

## Why orthographies become deep

Four mechanisms appear in the sources read, and they are cumulative rather than competing.

1. **Sound change after a spelling standard is fixed.** Frost's phonological source of opacity: the spoken language moves and the letters do not. Baroni's case is English, where a common orthographic standard was established around 1400 and the Great Vowel Shift then changed the pronunciation of almost all vowels, ending around 1600 (p. 137).
2. **Preservation of [morphology](morphology.md).** Frost notes that an orthography may be "designed to preserve and convey the morphologic information", so that a shared morpheme keeps a shared spelling across a sound alternation: *heal* and *health* (p. 278). Baroni observes that written French carries grammatical distinctions that speech has lost, with *parle*, *parles* and *parlent* all pronounced alike (p. 140).
3. **Etymological respelling.** Baroni records letters inserted between the Renaissance and the eighteenth century to display a word's ancestry: *cors*, *langage*, *doute* and *samon* became *corps*, *language*, *doubt* and *salmon* (p. 138). In French the same practice conferred the prestige of Latin.
4. **Interdialectal compromise.** Baroni attributes Danish depth to thirteenth and fourteenth century scribes choosing forms that reflected no single dialect, which preserved archaic pronunciations in the spelling (p. 141).

The first three all describe an orthography that was once shallower. Depth is generally accumulated rather than designed.

## The orthographic depth hypothesis

The claim that these differences change how reading works, not merely how hard it is.

Frost states it as the proposal "that shallow orthographies can easily support a word-recognition process that involves the printed word's phonology", because the phonology can be recovered from the print by simple computation, "in contrast, in deep orthographies like English or Hebrew, readers are encouraged to process printed words making use of larger units" (p. 281).

He then distinguishes two versions and rejects one of them.

- The **strong** form holds that in a shallow orthography the phonological representation is derived exclusively by prelexical translation, with no lexical involvement. Frost writes that it "is easy to show that the strong form of the Orthographic Depth Hypothesis is untenable": even Spanish, Italian and Serbo-Croatian fail to represent stress, and stress is not always predictable, so lexical knowledge is required for correct pronunciation in exactly the orthographies the strong form should fit best (p. 282).
- The **weak** form, which he reports gained increasing support, holds that both prelexical and lexical routes operate in every orthography, and that depth shifts the balance between them. The difference between deep and shallow is then "mainly quantitative, not qualitative".

## Evidence from learning to read

[Seymour et al. 2003](../references/seymour-et-al-2003.md) assessed letter knowledge, familiar word reading and simple nonword reading across English and twelve other European orthographies. Its abstract reports that children in most of those countries "become accurate and fluent in foundation level reading before the end of the first school year", with exceptions "notably in French, Portuguese, Danish, and, particularly, in English", and that "the rate of development in English is more than twice as slow as in the shallow orthographies".

Two qualifications belong with that figure. The authors separate depth from a second variable, reporting that "syllabic complexity selectively affects decoding, whereas orthographic depth affects both word reading and nonword reading", so depth is not the only thing being measured. And the mechanism they offer, that deep orthographies induce a dual logographic and alphabetic foundation, is stated in the abstract as a hypothesis rather than as a result.

## The case for depth

Deep orthographies are not simply defective, and the strongest counter-argument is old.

Baroni reports Chomsky and Halle's position of 1968 that English orthography represents the underlying form of lexemes well, and that an optimal orthography would be deep, holding a single representation for each lexical entry. On that view a shallow orthography such as Spanish is the inferior one, because it writes a predictable alternation that the reader could have computed (p. 138).

Baroni does not accept it, on two grounds: that native speakers are unlikely to have such detailed awareness of their own phonology, and that the supposed depth fails to appear where the argument predicts it, in *speak* against *speech* or *collide* against *collision*.

A second argument is functional. A deep orthography distinguishes homophones in writing, so *rite*, *write*, *right* and *Wright* are visually distinct, and written French marks number and gender that speech does not. Baroni quotes Berry's 1977 observation that a reader reads fluent English "efficiently only insofar as he treats the written language as if it were ideographic" (p. 139).

## Child topics

- **Grapheme-phoneme correspondence rules**, and how regularity and consistency are measured.
- **The psycholinguistic grain size theory**, named in the literature as a successor framing and not covered here.
- **Dual-route and strong phonological models of reading**, which the hypothesis's predictions depend on. Frost pp. 275 and following treats both.
- **Script reform**, the deliberate reduction of depth by policy.
- **Spelling as distinct from reading**, given that opacity has a direction.

## Open questions

- **Katz and Frost 1992 has not been read.** It is the chapter that named the hypothesis, and everything here about the strong form comes from Frost's later summary of it. Its bibliographic details, taken from citation aggregators rather than seen, are recorded in [Frost 2005](../references/frost-2005.md).
- **Seymour et al. 2003 was read only in abstract.** The thirteen orthographies are not named there and no per-language figures are given, so the comparison rests on the authors' own summary of their result.
- **No source read quantifies depth.** Every treatment consulted orders languages informally, and none gives a measure that would place a language on the continuum. Whether a standard metric exists is unknown here; if one does, it would be worth holding, since an informal ordering cannot be checked.
- **The evidence read is entirely alphabetic.** Depth as defined applies to grapheme-phoneme mapping, and how the notion transfers to a logosyllabary is not addressed by any source read. Frost's Hebrew case suggests the question is live, since the depth there is a consequence of the writing system's type rather than of its spelling.
- **The mechanisms of accumulation are drawn from a small set of languages.** English, French, Danish, Finnish and Italian, all European and all written in the Latin script. Whether the same four mechanisms account for depth elsewhere is not established.
