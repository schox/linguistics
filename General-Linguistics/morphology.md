# Morphology

The study of how words are put together. [Anderson et al.](../references/anderson-et-al-2022.md) start from *cats*, two pieces: *cat*, which picks out an animal, and *-s*, which says there is more than one.

The unit is the **morpheme**, which they define as the smallest systematic pairing of form, whether sound or sign, with meaning or grammatical function. The "or grammatical function" is doing real work: some morphemes mean something and others only mark something.

Order is not free. *cat* plus *-s* gives *cats*; the reverse gives *scat*, which is a word of English but is not those two pieces in the other order. It is a different word.

## Languages differ enormously in how much they put in a word

Most English words have one or two pieces. Anderson et al. count at least five in *non-renewability*.

Their contrasting example is the one worth carrying, because it is the one that constrains this vault's arithmetic. In the Harvaqtuurmiutut variety of Inuktitut, *iglujjualiulauqtuq* has five pieces and translates into English as a whole sentence, "They (sg) made a big house": *iglu* house, *-jjua* big, *-liu* make, *-lauq* distant past, *-tuq* declarative. They attribute the example to a 2010 paper by Richard Compton and Christine Pittman.

**One language's word is another language's sentence.** That is a commonplace of typology and a problem for this vault, developed below.

## What is a word, and why the vault should care

Anderson et al. are unusually blunt: the definition of "word" is "a hotly debated topic in linguistics". Linguists distinguish **phonological**, **morphological** and **syntactic** words and disagree about where the boundaries fall.

The everyday definition, something with spaces on either side, is **orthographic**, and they reject it for two reasons. Writing is not a reliable guide to a language's structure, and not every language is written with spaces or written at all.

Their working definition is the **smallest separable unit**: the smallest thing that can stand alone in an utterance, evidenced by one-word answers to questions and by the fact that words move around a sentence while their internal order stays fixed.

Note what that definition needs. It needs utterances, speakers, and the ability to ask a question and get an answer.

## Kober did morphology on a script nobody could read

[Alice Kober](../people/alice-kober.md) identified triplets in [Linear B](../Decipherment/linear-b.md): sets of sign groups sharing a stem and differing in their endings, which is inflection. She established that the language inflected, and which signs shared a consonant or a vowel, without knowing what any of it meant.

That is morphological analysis carried out with no meanings and no speakers, from distribution alone, and it is the single most productive thing anyone did to Linear B before Ventris. The vault has referred to the triplets repeatedly and has never said that what she was doing has a name.

Morphology is unusually available to a decipherer, for a reason worth stating. Inflectional paradigms are **repetition with systematic variation**, and that is a pattern visible in the signs themselves. It does not require knowing the language; it requires enough text for the paradigm to recur. This is [redundancy](redundancy.md) in the form a decipherer can actually use.

## The caveat this puts on the vault's central comparison

**This is the vault's own reasoning and is not drawn from a source.**

The Decipherment area's core argument rests on mean signs per document: [Linear A](../Decipherment/linear-a.md) at about 5.2, the [Indus](../Decipherment/indus-script.md) corpus at 4.6, [Linear B](../Decipherment/linear-b.md) at most about 12.5. The unit is the document, not the word, and the vault chose that unit because word boundaries in an unread script are not identifiable. That was the right choice and it has a consequence the vault has not stated.

If one language can pack into a single word what another spreads across a sentence, then a five-sign document in an unread script could be a list of five items or a single heavily inflected clause, and nothing in the sign count distinguishes them. **Signs per document is not a measure of how much language is present.** It is a measure of how much writing is present, and the conversion rate between the two is a property of the language, which is unknown by hypothesis.

This compounds the tension already recorded in [redundancy](redundancy.md), where repetitive corpora were argued to carry less information than their token counts imply. Both point the same way: the vault's cross-script comparisons are more generous to short corpora than they should be. Neither is settled, and both are stated here as problems.

## Open questions

- **Derivation versus inflection is not covered**, and it is the basic division of the subject. The source has separate sections on each, on allomorphy, on compounding and on morphological trees, none of which is used here.
- **The morphological typology is missing entirely**: isolating, agglutinative, fusional, polysynthetic. The vault invokes the Inuktitut case without the category it exemplifies, and the typology belongs under **Typology and universals** with a proper source rather than being smuggled in here.
- **The Compton and Pittman paper is not held as a Reference** and has not been consulted; the Inuktitut example reaches the vault entirely through a textbook.
- **The claim that signs per document is not a measure of language quantity has not been checked against anyone who has made it.** It is an obvious enough point that it will have been made, and the vault should find where.
- **Nothing here covers how morphological analysis is actually done on a corpus**, which is the operational question for both decipherment and computational work: paradigm discovery, unsupervised segmentation, and the fact that both are the same problem as [what counts as one sign](../Decipherment/segmentation-and-transcription.md).
