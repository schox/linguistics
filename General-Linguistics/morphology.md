# Morphology

The study of how words are put together. [Anderson et al. 2022](../references/anderson-et-al-2022.md) define it that way and start from *cats*, which has two pieces: *cat*, which picks out a kind of animal, and *-s*, which indicates more than one of them.

## The morpheme

The unit is the **morpheme**: the smallest systematic pairing of a form, whether sound or sign, with a meaning or a grammatical function.

The disjunction matters. Some morphemes carry a meaning of the kind lexical semantics deals with; others express abstract grammatical information and cannot be glossed as a meaning at all.

Morphemes cannot always stand alone. *cat* can; *-s* cannot. Words are the smallest *separable* units, but not the smallest units.

Order is fixed. *cat* plus *-s* gives *cats*. The reverse gives *scat*, which is a word of English but is not those two pieces reversed; it is an unrelated word.

## How much a word can carry

Most English words have one or two morphemes. Anderson et al. count at least five in *non-renewability*: *non-*, *re-*, *new*, *-abil* and *-ity*.

Languages differ enormously in this. In the Harvaqtuurmiutut variety of Inuktitut, the word *iglujjualiulauqtuq* has five pieces and translates into English as a whole sentence, "They (sg) made a big house":

| Piece | Function |
| --- | --- |
| *iglu* | house |
| *-jjua* | big |
| *-liu* | make |
| *-lauq* | distant past |
| *-tuq* | declarative |

Anderson et al. attribute the example to a 2010 paper by Richard Compton and Christine Pittman.

## What counts as a word

Anderson et al. describe this as "a hotly debated topic in linguistics".

The everyday definition, something written with spaces on either side, is **orthographic**, and they reject it on two grounds: writing is not a reliable guide to a language's structure, and not every language is written with spaces or written at all.

Linguists instead distinguish:

- **Phonological words**, for the purposes of sound patterns
- **Morphological words**, for the purposes of word structure
- **Syntactic words**, for the purposes of sentence structure

These do not always coincide, and their boundaries are disputed.

The working definition Anderson et al. adopt is the **smallest separable unit**: the smallest thing that can stand on its own in an utterance. The evidence is that content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs) can serve as one-word answers to questions, and that words are **syntactically independent**, moving to different positions in a sentence while their internal order stays fixed.

## The main divisions

Named here so the child notes have a frame; each is treated in its own section of the source and none is described in detail in this note.

- **Roots, bases and affixes.** The structural pieces a word is built from.
- **Allomorphy.** One morpheme with more than one form.
- **Lexical categories.** Noun, verb, adjective, adverb and the rest.
- **Derivational morphology.** Building a new word, often of a different category.
- **Inflectional morphology.** Marking grammatical information on an existing word, such as subject agreement or tense, without making a new one.
- **Compounding.** Combining two words, with one acting as the **head**.
- **Morphological trees.** Notating the internal structure, including where a word is structurally ambiguous.

## Open questions

- **Derivation and inflection are named and not described.** They are the basic division of the subject and each has its own section in the source.
- **The morphological typology is absent**: isolating, agglutinative, fusional and polysynthetic. The Inuktitut example above illustrates a category this vault has not written down, and it belongs under **Typology and universals** with a typological source rather than a textbook.
- **The Compton and Pittman paper is not held as a Reference** and has not been consulted. The Inuktitut example reaches the vault at second hand.
- **Nothing here covers morphological analysis as a procedure**, which is how the subject is actually practiced on unfamiliar data.
