# Akkadian

The Semitic language of Mesopotamia, written in [cuneiform](../Decipherment/cuneiform.md) from roughly the middle of the third millennium BCE until around the first century CE, with Babylonian and Assyrian as its major dialects.

## Why it mattered for the decipherment

Akkadian is the case where a **known family** did the work that a known descendant language did for [Egyptian](egyptian.md).

Nobody had a late readable form of Akkadian the way Coptic preserved Egyptian. What they had instead was Semitic comparison: Hebrew, Arabic and Aramaic were well studied, and once the script yielded enough phonetic values the language behind it could be recognized as a relative and analyzed with an existing comparative apparatus. That is the *different script, different language* case in the vault's problem space, and it is tractable exactly because a related known language exists.

Set that against [Linear A](../Decipherment/linear-a.md), where no relative has been established, and the contrast is the whole reason the vault keeps the two questions apart.

## The borrowed script problem

Akkadian scribes adopted a writing system built for [Sumerian](sumerian.md), a language unrelated to theirs in structure as well as in family. Mnamon's Akkadian entry describes the result as a mixed system of syllables and logograms with around 600 signs in modern sign lists.

Borrowing is where the polyvalence comes from. A sign that stood for a Sumerian word could be kept as a logogram, or taken for its sound and reused syllabically, and both uses survive side by side. That is the homophony and polyphony that made nineteenth-century scholars doubt the decipherment, and it is a general consequence of adapting a script to a language it was not designed for rather than a peculiarity of Mesopotamia. See [what counts as one sign](../Decipherment/segmentation-and-transcription.md).
