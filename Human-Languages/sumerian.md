# Sumerian

The language of ancient Sumer, an isolate with no established relatives, and the language [cuneiform](../Decipherment/cuneiform.md) was invented to write.

## An isolate that was read anyway

Sumerian is the counter-example the vault needs, and it complicates the story the other notes tell.

Every undeciphered script in this vault suffers from some version of "no known relative": [Linear A](../Decipherment/linear-a.md) has no identified language, the [Indus script](../Decipherment/indus-script.md) may encode none, [Cypro-Minoan](../Decipherment/cypro-minoan.md)'s is unidentified. Sumerian is an isolate too. It has no known relatives, no descendant, and a structure unlike the Semitic languages around it. And it is read.

What made the difference was not relatedness but **bilingual tradition**. Akkadian scribes learned Sumerian as a classical language and left sign lists, vocabularies and interlinear translations. The language survives with its own key attached, produced by people who used it, which is a resource no undeciphered script here has. Access to a language does not require a relative if the ancients left a dictionary.

That is worth holding against the vault's usual framing, which treats a candidate relative as the thing that unlocks a corpus. It is one route, not the only one.

## Discovery by inference

Torres Torres records that when Botta rediscovered Nineveh and Ashurbanipal's library in 1842, with tens of thousands of tablets, **Edward Hincks** inferred correctly that the writing had been invented by an earlier Mesopotamian people who bequeathed it to later cultures, people Julius Oppert would afterwards name the Sumerians.

So the existence of the language was deduced from properties of the script before the people were identified. Signs that behaved as logograms with values that made no sense in Akkadian implied a prior language, and the inference held.
