# Basque

Basque, *Euskara* to its speakers, is a living language of the western Pyrenees, spoken in northern Spain and southwestern France. It is the only language of western Europe with no demonstrated relative, living or dead, and the only one that survives from before the spread of Indo-European into the region.

## Classification

Glottolog classifies Basque as an isolate: a family of one, with no parent languoid, citing Trask 1997 for the family membership. Its glottocode is `basq1248` and its ISO 639-3 code is `eus`. WALS holds it under a family and a genus both named Basque, with the code `bsq`.

Relationships to other languages have been proposed repeatedly and none is accepted in the classifications held here. What Glottolog records is the absence of a parent, which is a statement about the evidence rather than a claim that no relative ever existed.

Basque appears in both of the samples WALS defines, the 100-language sample and the 200-language sample. That is a consequence of how those samples are built: they maximize genealogical and areal diversity, and a family of one contributes a whole branch by itself.

## Attestation

Basque is attested continuously in the modern period and sparsely before it, which makes its early record a matter of a few objects rather than a corpus.

The Hand of Irulegi is the current earliest substantial candidate. [Aiestaran and colleagues](../references/aiestaran-et-al-2024.md) report an inscribed bronze hand recovered in 2021 from an Iron Age settlement in the Ebro Valley in Navarre, which they date to the first century BC on radiocarbon determinations spanning 146 BC to AD 67 and 334 to 2 BC. They identify eighteen distinct signs across four lines and read words including *sorioneku*, *kunekebe* and *eŕaukon*. Their argument is that the language is Vasconic, sharing its roots with modern Basque, and that the text is the first example of Vasconic epigraphy. That is their claim and it rests on a short text from a single object.

The script is a separate question from the language, and the same paper separates them: the signs are a Palaeohispanic semi-syllabary, which the authors argue is a distinct sub-system rather than the Celtiberian one. A reader can transliterate the inscription without thereby understanding it, which is the distinction [Etruscan](etruscan.md) demonstrates at greater length.

The paper also records what the find unsettles. Few examples of Palaeohispanic writing had been recovered from Vasconic territories, "leading to the assumption that the Vascones were a pre-literate society", in the authors' words.

## Structure

The sketch below is drawn from WALS, which codes each value against named grammars; those grammars are named here because the claim is theirs.

Basque is verb-final. WALS records the order of subject, object and verb as SOV, with postpositions rather than prepositions, genitive before noun and adjective after noun, on Lafitte 1962, Saltarelli and colleagues 1988, Rebuschi 1984, Urbina 1989 and Hualde and Ortiz de Urbina 2003.

The sound system is unremarkable in size: consonant inventory average, vowel quality inventory average at five or six, consonant-to-vowel ratio moderately high, all on Saltarelli and colleagues 1988.

Its morphology is where it is distinctive. WALS records ten or more cases, marked by postpositional clitics rather than by suffixes proper, and no grammatical gender, on Lafitte 1962, Saltarelli and colleagues 1988 and Trask 1995. Inflection is equally prefixing and suffixing, which is uncommon and which WALS codes on Hualde and Ortiz de Urbina 2003.

Alignment is the point of interest and WALS does not give one answer to it. Case marking of full noun phrases is coded active-inactive, on Hualde and Ortiz de Urbina 2003; alignment of verbal person marking is coded ergative, on Saltarelli and colleagues 1988. Basque is conventionally described as an ergative language, and the two codings are not a contradiction so much as a report that the two systems are aligned differently. Which description is right is not settled here.

## Open questions

- **No speaker figure is recorded here.** Ethnologue holds the standard count and sells it; its free pages name only the four largest languages. The Basque Government's periodic sociolinguistic survey is the obvious alternative and was not consulted for this note.
- **Trask 1997 is cited by Glottolog and has not been read here.** It is the reference behind the isolate classification, and the classification is stated on Glottolog's authority rather than on the book's.
- **The Aquitanian material is not covered**, and no source was read for it. It is usually described as the ancestor or a close relative of Basque, and whether the Irulegi text changes that picture is not something the sources read here address.
- **Nothing here covers the proposed external relationships**, including the Vasconic substrate hypothesis and comparisons to Iberian and to North Caucasian. Attributing those properly needs their proponents and their critics, and neither was read.
