# Nivkh

Nivkh, also called Gilyak in older literature, is spoken on Sakhalin and along the lower Amur in the Russian Far East. Like [Ainu](ainu.md) it is a small family rather than a single language, and like Ainu it has no demonstrated relative.

## Classification

Glottolog treats Nivkh as an independent family, glottocode `nivk1234`, holding two languages and five dialects. The two languages are Amur Nivkh (`gily1242`, which carries the ISO 639-3 code `niv`, with Amur-West Sakhalin Nivkh and North Sakhalin Nivkh beneath it) and Nighvng (`sakh1247`, with Central, East and South Sakhalin Nivkh beneath it).

On the question of a wider relationship Glottolog is explicit, and the wording is worth quoting because it states a negative result rather than an absence of interest: "Parallels to Chukotko-Kamchatkan are insufficient to conclude a genealogical relationship."

The identifiers on this note point at the family for the glottocode and at Amur Nivkh for the ISO code, because that is how the two registries divide the ground. The mismatch is recorded rather than smoothed over, and it is the same problem [Ainu](ainu.md) raises: a name in common use covers a group that the classifications split.

WALS holds Nivkh under the code `niv` and includes it in the 200-language sample but not the 100-language sample.

## Attestation

**Not covered here, for want of a source read.** Nivkh has no early written record; a Cyrillic-based orthography exists for it in the modern period. The descriptive record rests on twentieth-century fieldwork, principally the works WALS cites, and none was read for this note.

## Structure

The sketch below is drawn from WALS, which codes each value against named grammars; those grammars are named here because the claim is theirs.

Nivkh is verb-final, with SOV order, postpositions, genitive before noun and adjective before noun, coded on Panfilov 1962 and Comrie 1980.

Its sound system is moderately large: WALS codes the consonant inventory as moderately large, the vowel quality inventory as average at five or six, and the consonant-to-vowel ratio as moderately high, on Gruzdeva 1998 and Krejnovich 1937.

The morphology is suffixing and case-marked, and the sources WALS uses do not fully agree about the cases. The feature on the number of cases records eight or nine, on Gruzdeva 1998, marked by case suffixes; the feature on the exponence of inflectional formatives records no case at all, on Austerlitz, Gruzdeva 1998, and Panfilov 1962 and 1968. Both codings appear in WALS against the same language, and no attempt is made here to decide between them. There is no grammatical gender.

Alignment is neutral on both measures WALS applies, for case marking of full noun phrases and for verbal person marking, on Gruzdeva 1998. Pronominal subjects are optional pronouns in subject position rather than affixes on the verb, on Panfilov 1962, which sets Nivkh apart from the other three isolates covered here, all of which mark the subject on the verb. Negation is affixal, with WALS coding both negation features as optional double negation, on Panfilov 1962.

## Open questions

- **No speaker figure is recorded here**, and Nivkh is severely endangered, so the figure matters and moves. Ethnologue's count is behind a paywall.
- **WALS carries two incompatible codings of case** for this language, noted above. Which is right depends on what counts as a case in Nivkh, and settling it needs Gruzdeva 1998 and Panfilov 1962 read directly rather than through WALS.
- **Whether "Nivkh" names one language or two** is answered differently by the two registries used here, and the answer decides whether this note should eventually become two.
- **The Chukotko-Kamchatkan comparison** is recorded above as Glottolog states it. The primary literature making and rejecting that comparison has not been read.
