# Edward Hincks

Irish clergyman and Assyriologist (19 August 1792 to 3 December 1866), rector of Killyleagh in County Down, and the person who worked out how [cuneiform](../Decipherment/cuneiform.md) actually functions.

## The findings

Torres Torres credits him with being the first to reach the important conclusions about the Akkadian language in cuneiform: that the writing was essentially syllabic, with both open and closed syllables; that one character could carry several values; and that some signs were logograms, which he detected and whose values he established.

That list is the structure of the script. Everything the vault says in [cuneiform](../Decipherment/cuneiform.md) about polyvalence, and everything the sceptics of the 1850s objected to, is a description of what Hincks found.

He also made the inference that produced [Sumerian](../Human-Languages/sumerian.md). When Botta's work at Nineveh in 1842 brought Ashurbanipal's library to light, with tens of thousands of tablets, Hincks concluded that the script had been invented by an earlier Mesopotamian people who passed it to later cultures. Oppert later named them the Sumerians. The people were deduced from properties of their writing before they were identified.

He was one of the four in the **1857 test**, and Torres Torres records that his translation and Rawlinson's were the closest of the four.

## Why the note exists

He satisfies the inclusion test twice over, but the reason to write him is the vault's rule about credit.

Torres Torres's judgment, translated from the Spanish, is that some of the most significant discoveries about cuneiform writing and the Akkadian language are owed to Hincks, who stands in the unjust shadow of the just fame achieved by [Rawlinson](rawlinson.md).

The pattern is familiar here. [Alice Kober](alice-kober.md) established the structure of [Linear B](../Decipherment/linear-b.md) without assigning a sound value and is remembered less than Ventris. Hincks established the structure of cuneiform and is remembered less than Rawlinson. In both cases the structural work came first and made the announcement possible, and in both cases the announcement is what the name attaches to.

There are differences worth keeping. Kober died before the decipherment and could not defend her claim; Hincks lived to 1866, took part in the 1857 test on equal terms, and published throughout. His relative obscurity is not a matter of dying early but of where he worked and what he published in: a country rectory in Ireland, and the *Transactions of the Royal Irish Academy*.

## Before cuneiform

He studied Egyptian hieroglyphs first, and Torres Torres notes he made significant contributions there too. That makes him a second link between the two decipherments this vault has now covered, alongside the shared method of starting from proper names.

## Open questions

- **None of his papers has been read.** They are in the *Transactions of the Royal Irish Academy* and long out of copyright. For a note whose whole claim is that his findings were foundational, resting on one secondary account is thin.
- **His Egyptological work is mentioned and not described.** Whether it fed his cuneiform method, which would be the interesting thing, is not established.
- **The specific attributions disputed between him and Rawlinson are not itemized here**, because the source asserts the general judgment rather than listing the cases.
