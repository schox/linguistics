# Alice Kober

American classicist (23 December 1906 to 16 May 1950), who taught at Brooklyn College from 1930 until her death, becoming assistant professor in 1936 and associate professor in the year she died, aged 43.

## The work

Kober analyzed Linear B distributionally, without attempting to assign sound values, and this restraint is the whole point of her method. By tracking sign groups that varied only in their endings, she identified sets of related forms (her "triplets") and the phenomenon of **bridging syllables**, where a single sign spans the end of a root and the start of an inflectional suffix. Because Linear B is a syllabary, the sign at that boundary changes when the ending changes, and mapping which signs alternate with which established phonetic *relationships* between signs while their actual values remained unknown.

In three papers (1945, 1946, 1948) she used this to demonstrate that the script recorded an **inflected language**. That is a structural finding extracted from distribution alone, and it is the direct ancestor of Ventris's grid.

She worked from roughly 180,000 hand-cut index cards, made from scrap paper in wartime conditions, and forty notebooks.

## Why she matters here

Kober is the clearest demonstration that the first useful move on an undeciphered script is structural rather than semantic. She was not guessing at meaning. She was asking what the distribution could be made to prove, which is the same discipline that governs [frequency analysis](../Decipherment/frequency-analysis.md) and the modern computational work.

She is also the field's standing example of misattributed credit. She died two years before the decipherment was announced, and popular accounts have overwhelmingly been about Ventris. [Chadwick](john-chadwick.md) judged her preparatory work the most valuable that preceded the solution, and an obituary predicted her "careful and faithful spade-work" would prove essential, which it did.
