# Jean-François Champollion

French scholar (1790-1832), a teacher at Grenoble when he began corresponding with Thomas Young about the Rosetta Stone in 1814, and the person who read [Egyptian hieroglyphs](../Decipherment/egyptian-hieroglyphs.md), the script of the [Egyptian](../Human-Languages/egyptian.md) language.

## What he actually did

The popular version has him decipher the Rosetta Stone. The precise version is better and is what the vault should carry.

In 1822 he saw copies of the Philae obelisk inscriptions, where William John Bankes had tentatively noted the names Ptolemaios and Kleopatra in both Greek and hieroglyphic. From those he read the phonetic characters of `k l e o p a t r a`, and building on them and the foreign names on the Rosetta Stone he constructed an alphabet of phonetic hieroglyphs. That is the *Lettre à M. Dacier*, addressed to the secretary of the Académie des Inscriptions et Belles-Lettres and published immediately in 1822.

**The step that mattered is in the postscript.** He noted there that the same phonetic characters appeared to occur in both Greek and Egyptian names, which would mean the phonetic principle was native to the script rather than a Ptolemaic contrivance for spelling foreign words. He confirmed it in 1823, identifying Ramesses and Thutmose in cartouches at Abu Simbel, from copies made by Bankes and sent to him by Jean-Nicolas Huyot.

He then spent the rest of a short life on the grammar and the dictionary, both published after his death in 1832.

## The dispute with Young, recorded rather than settled

The vault's convention is that credit is part of the record, so the dispute belongs here in its particulars.

**Young's contribution was real.** Prompted by Silvestre de Sacy in 1814 to look for cartouches containing Greek names, he found the phonetic characters for Ptolemaios in the hieroglyphic text, noticed they resembled their demotic equivalents, and identified as many as 80 similarities between the hieroglyphic and demotic scripts, which had been thought entirely distinct. From that he correctly deduced that demotic was only partly phonetic and partly ideographic, with signs derived from hieroglyphs. This appeared in his article "Egypt" in the *Encyclopædia Britannica* in 1819. Dalby's assessment is that he "could make no further progress, however".

**The quarrel was national and bitter.** Champollion acknowledged Young in the 1822 *Lettre*, but incompletely according to early British critics. James Browne, a sub-editor at the *Encyclopædia Britannica*, anonymously wrote a series of articles in the *Edinburgh Review* in 1823 praising Young and alleging that the "unscrupulous" Champollion had plagiarized him; these were translated into French by Klaproth and published as a book in 1827. Young reasserted his own contribution in 1823. The deaths of Young in 1829 and Champollion in 1832 did not end it, and Wallis Budge was still giving Young special emphasis in 1904.

The vault takes no position on precedence. What it can say is that the two men solved different problems: Young established that hieroglyphs could spell foreign names phonetically, and Champollion established that they spelled Egyptian ones, which is the finding that made the script readable. Both are in the sequence recorded on the script note.

There is also a documented case of actual plagiarism in the story, and it runs the other way. Champollion's former student François Salvolini died in 1838, and Champollion's missing analytical drafts were found among his papers, showing that Salvolini's own 1823 publication on the stone had been taken from them.

## Why he is here

He is the standard label for the decipherment, which satisfies the second limb of the inclusion test in `types/person.md` on its own. He is also the clearest instance in the vault of a decipherment turning on a conceptual move rather than on new evidence: the material he needed had been in print and circulating since 1802.

## Open questions

- **Nothing here is from his own writing.** The *Lettre à M. Dacier* is short, published in 1822, and long out of copyright, so there is no good reason for this note to rest entirely on Dalby's summary.
- **His life is barely covered.** Born 1790, teaching at Grenoble by 1814, dead at 41 in 1832. His Coptic learning, which is what let him use the language rather than only the signs, is not treated at all and is the part most relevant to this vault.
- **Thomas Young has no note**, and on the evidence above he meets the inclusion test. He also bridges areas, having worked on physics and physiology, which is exactly the cross-area signal `field` is meant to capture.
