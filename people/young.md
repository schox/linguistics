# Thomas Young

English polymath (1773-1829), and the other half of the [Egyptian](../Decipherment/egyptian-hieroglyphs.md) decipherment quarrel. Owed a note since the vault wrote [Champollion](champollion.md), where the dispute is recorded in detail and only one side has a name attached to it.

## What he established

Silvestre de Sacy wrote to him in 1814 suggesting he look in the hieroglyphic text for cartouches that ought to contain Greek names, and try to identify phonetic characters in them. Dalby records what followed.

Young found the phonetic characters spelling **Ptolemaios**, read in his transliteration as `p t o l m e s`. He noticed they resembled their equivalents in the demotic, and went on to identify **as many as 80 similarities** between the hieroglyphic and demotic scripts, which had been thought entirely distinct. From that he deduced, correctly, that demotic was only partly phonetic and partly ideographic, with signs derived from hieroglyphs.

This appeared in his long article "Egypt" for the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* in 1819.

Dalby's assessment of what came next is one sentence: "He could make no further progress, however."

## Why the vault holds both him and Champollion

They solved different problems, and the vault's position is that the distinction is clearer than the quarrel.

Young established that hieroglyphs **could** spell foreign names phonetically. [Champollion](champollion.md) established that they spelled **Egyptian** names phonetically, which is the finding that made the script readable, because it removed the escape route by which the phonetic principle could be dismissed as a Ptolemaic contrivance for writing Greek.

The vault takes no position on precedence. It does note that Young's contribution was real, was acknowledged in the 1822 *Lettre à M. Dacier* though incompletely by British lights, and that the ferocity of what followed had rather more to do with nationality than with either man's evidence. James Browne wrote anonymously in the *Edinburgh Review* in 1823 alleging that the "unscrupulous" Champollion had plagiarized him; Young reasserted his own claim the same year; Wallis Budge was still pressing the case in 1904.

## A polymath, with a caveat

Wikidata describes him as an English polymath, and he is remembered outside this field for work in physics and physiology.

That would satisfy the third limb of the inclusion test in `types/person.md`, which asks whether someone bridges two or more areas of the vault. **The vault does not assert it here**, because nothing read for this note covers his scientific work, and "polymath" in a database description is not a source for what he actually did. If the bridge is real it should be written from something that describes it.

## Open questions

- **Nothing of his has been read.** The 1819 *Britannica* article is long out of copyright and is the primary record of everything above.
- **His non-Egyptological work is unexamined**, which is the interesting question for this vault rather than a biographical gap: whether the man who did the wave-theory work brought anything from it to the cartouches.
- **The note rests on a single source.** Dalby 2019 supplies the decipherment sequence and the dispute; there is no second account here, and Dalby is writing primarily about the Rosetta Stone rather than about Young.
