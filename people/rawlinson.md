# Henry Creswicke Rawlinson

British officer, diplomat and Assyriologist (11 April 1810 to 5 March 1895), who copied the [Behistun](../places/behistun.md) inscription and did more than anyone to make [cuneiform](../Decipherment/cuneiform.md) readable.

## The career that made the work possible

Torres Torres's account: born at Chadlington in Oxfordshire, out to India in 1827 as a cadet with the East India Company, then sent to Persia to help organize the shah's army, where he mastered Persian and several Indian languages and began working on the undeciphered inscriptions around him. Later political agent at Kandahar and in Ottoman Arabia, and from 1843 consul at Baghdad, where he devoted himself to cuneiform. He took part in archaeological missions, including one with Austen Henry Layard at Nineveh. After twenty-two years in the East he returned to Britain in 1849, gave his collection of Near Eastern antiquities to the British Museum, and was commissioned by it to continue Layard's excavations; he was back in Baghdad from 1851 to 1855.

Afterwards a public career: director of the East India Company, twice a Member of Parliament, three times on the Council of India, a trustee of the British Museum from 1876 until his death, president of the Royal Geographical Society from 1874 to 1875 and of the Royal Asiatic Society from 1878 to 1881.

**The point of listing all that is that none of it is scholarship.** Rawlinson reached the material because the Company and the Foreign Office put him next to it, and the vault's Decipherment area has a recurring pattern of decipherment being done by people whose day job placed them near the evidence. [John Chadwick](john-chadwick.md) came to Linear B from wartime cryptanalysis; [Champollion](champollion.md) is the exception who was a scholar first.

## What he did

He arrived at values for Old Persian royal names independently of Grotefend, from short trilingual inscriptions at Mount Elwend. Then from 1835 he copied Darius's inscription at Behistun, which is long enough to work with, by lowering himself down a cliff face and staying there for hours.

Old Persian gave him a foothold; the trilingual gave him Babylonian alongside it; and by 1857 he was one of the four whose independent translations of the Tiglath-Pileser I prism were compared and found to agree. Torres Torres records that his version and Hincks's were the closest of the four.

## Credit, and the shadow he cast

The vault's rule is that credit is part of the record, and here the historiography itself says the credit was misallocated.

Torres Torres writes that some of the most significant discoveries about cuneiform writing and the Akkadian language are owed to [Edward Hincks](hincks.md), who stands "in the unjust shadow of the just fame achieved by Rawlinson" (translated from the Spanish; *a la injusta sombra de la justa fama lograda por Rawlinson*).

Both halves of that sentence matter. Rawlinson's fame is described as deserved. The shadow it cast is described as unjust. This is the same shape as [Alice Kober](alice-kober.md) beside Ventris, and it is worth noticing that the vault has now met it twice in four `Person` notes: the person who did the structural work is remembered less than the person who announced the result.

## Open questions

- **Nothing here is from his own writing.** *The Persian Cuneiform Inscription at Behistun* (1846-1851) is out of copyright and is the primary record of both the copying and the method.
- **The division of labor with Hincks is not established here.** Torres Torres asserts that Hincks made discoveries credited to Rawlinson but the specifics are in her footnotes rather than argued, and this note reports her judgment rather than testing it.
- **His role in the 1857 test is recorded and his conduct in it is not.** Talbot proposed the comparison; whether Rawlinson welcomed it is not stated in anything read.
- **`viaf` not looked up.**
