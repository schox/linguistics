# Georg Friedrich Grotefend

German epigraphist and philologist, whose work on the Persepolis inscriptions bore fruit in **1802**, twenty years before Champollion's *Lettre* and with no bilingual of any kind.

## What he did with two short inscriptions

Torres Torres gives the reasoning, and it is worth following because it is the purest case in the vault of getting somewhere from pattern alone.

He had two Old Persian inscriptions from Persepolis. He identified a recurring sign group as **"king"** and a longer one as **"king of kings"**. From the way the names and titles were arranged he inferred that the author of one text was the son of the author of the other. He then matched that pair against Darius and Xerxes, because Greek historiography said Persepolis was built by kings Herodotus had named.

With the names guessed, he assigned sound values from the Greek, Hebrew and Avestan forms of them, reading `d a r h e u s h` for Darius and `kh sh h e r sh e` for Xerxes. Applying those values to the group he had read as "king" produced `kh sh e h ? ? h`. Finding the royal title *khscheio* in Duperron's 1771 edition of the Avesta, he supposed the language of the inscriptions was Avestan, recovered the vowels he was missing, and transliterated Hystaspes, Darius's father, as `g o sh t a s p`.

## Wrong in the details, right in the principle

Several values were incorrect. Torres Torres notes that he assigned the value "o", which does not exist in the script, and that **he never discovered that the signs were not fully alphabetic**.

None of that mattered. The principle was right, and it is the same principle that worked at Rosetta twenty years later and at Ras Shamra a century and a quarter after that: **proper names are the place where a known thing and an unknown script are forced to touch.** [Cartouches](../Decipherment/egyptian-hieroglyphs.md) did the same work for [Egyptian](../Human-Languages/egyptian.md), and looking for a four-consonant word meaning "axe" did it for [Ugaritic](../Decipherment/ugaritic-script.md).

He also did it without a bilingual, which distinguishes him from every other case in this vault. Champollion had the [Rosetta](../places/rosetta.md) Stone, Rawlinson had [Behistun](../places/behistun.md), Bauer had a language family. Grotefend had two palace inscriptions, a Greek historian and a hunch about genealogy.

[Rawlinson](rawlinson.md) later reached comparable results independently from the trilingual inscriptions at Mount Elwend, and it was his Behistun copies that turned Grotefend's foothold into a decipherment.
