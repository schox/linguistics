# Linear A

The administrative script of Bronze Age Crete, used mainly on Crete and sporadically in mainland Greece and the Aegean islands, and undeciphered. Salgarella and Castellan describe it as logo-syllabic: signs standing for open syllables alongside signs standing for whole words or concepts.

`writes:` is deliberately empty. The language behind Linear A has not been identified, and that is the fact the note exists to record.

## The corpus, which is the whole argument

Petrolito and colleagues give 1,427 Linear A documents containing 7,362 to 7,396 signs, and set that against more than 4,600 Linear B documents containing 57,398 signs, attributing both counts to Younger.

Those two numbers are the vault's central claim in its most checkable form, and the two ratios between them are not the same, which is the part worth noticing.

By document count Linear A has roughly **31 percent** of Linear B's corpus. By sign count it has roughly **13 percent**. The gap is document length: about 5 signs per document in Linear A against about 12 in Linear B, on these figures. Linear A is not simply a smaller archive of the same kind of text, it is an archive of markedly shorter texts, and short texts are worse than proportionately unhelpful. Distributional argument needs contexts, and a five-sign administrative entry supplies very few.

(Both Linear B figures are lower bounds, since the source says "more than 4,600 documents"; the true ratios are therefore somewhat lower than stated.)

Set that against everything the two scripts share: the same island, overlapping findspots, a script family so close that Linear B was built on Linear A, and the same generation of scholars working on both. One is read and one is not, and the difference is very largely what happened to survive and be excavated. See [The evidentiary threshold and unicity distance](evidentiary-threshold-and-unicity-distance.md).

The corpus of record is [GORILA](../references/godart-olivier-1976.md), five print volumes published between 1976 and 1985 and never superseded. Its being print-only is not a bibliographic detail: Salgarella and Castellan built the SigLA database because a printed edition "does not allow to carry out any statistical and comparative analysis of signs and sign-sequences". An unreadable corpus and an uncountable one fail in different ways, and Linear A has both problems.

## The sign inventory

Counts differ because they count different things, and the difference is worth holding.

- Salgarella and Castellan, following the standard list in GORILA volume 5, give some 180 simple signs, some 164 complex or composite signs formed by combining simple ones, and some 30 fractional signs.
- [Rutter](../references/rutter-aegean-prehistory.md) describes a syllabary of "some 85 distinct signs".
- Petrolito and colleagues note that 81 signs are shared with Linear B and are assumed to carry syllabic values, the remainder being assumed logograms.

The 85 and the 180 are not in conflict. One is an estimate of the syllabic subset, the other the full inventory of simple signs. Quoting either alone as "the number of Linear A signs" produces an argument that does not survive contact with the other.

## Reading without understanding

Because many signs are shared with Linear B in the same or closely similar shapes, the phonetic values recovered from the Linear B decipherment can be applied to them. That yields an approximate pronunciation of Linear A sign-sequences without yielding any meaning.

This is the cleanest illustration in the vault of [script versus language](script-versus-language.md) as two separate questions. Linear A is partly readable and wholly not understood. It sits in the fourth case of the problem space, where neither script nor language is known, which is the case for which no established method exists.

## Candidate languages, none established

Petrolito and colleagues survey the hypotheses, and they are reported here as positions held rather than as findings. Greek-like (Nagy 1963), a distinct Indo-European branch (Owens 1999), Anatolian and close to Luvian (Palmer 1958), an archaic form of Phoenician (Dietrich and Loretz 2001), Indo-Iranian (Faure 1998), and Etruscan-like (Facchetti and Negri 2003).

Salgarella and Castellan state the negative case more usefully than any of these state a positive one: the language "does not seem to be related to any of the Indo-European languages so far known (most notably Greek), nor does it to Semitic ones". The conventional label for it is Minoan, which names the problem rather than solving it.

Under the vault's rule on contested claims, none of the six is endorsed here. The reason to record them is that the pattern is itself informative: a script with an unidentified language attracts proposals faster than it can test them, which is the point made in [Is it writing at all?](is-it-writing-at-all.md) about a different corpus.

## Dating

The range in the frontmatter, c. 1800-1450 BCE, follows Salgarella and Castellan. Rutter puts the first appearance at MM IIA or perhaps MM IB and the latest inscriptions no later than LM I, which is compatible. Petrolito and colleagues give 2500-1450 BC in their Table 1, several centuries earlier at the start and not obviously supported elsewhere; it is treated here as an outlier rather than as a live dispute.

## Open questions

- **The archaeological distribution is thinly sourced here.** Rutter gives Ayia Triadha 168 tablets and names Archanes, Knossos, Mallia, Phaistos, Zakro and Chania as further findspots without per-site counts. A per-site breakdown of the 1,427 documents would make the provenance argument in [Hagia Triada](../places/hagia-triada.md) quantitative rather than comparative. It was not found in any source read.
- **Younger's transcriptions could not be reached.** Both Petrolito and colleagues and Salgarella and Castellan treat John G. Younger's Linear A texts site at the University of Kansas as the best available digital transcription, and the corpus figures used here descend from it. The host `people.ku.edu` did not resolve on 2026-08-09. Whether the site has moved, been retired, or was temporarily unavailable was not established. If it is gone, the vault is relying on figures whose primary presentation no longer exists.
- **`class: mixed` here against `class: syllabary` on Linear B.** Both scripts are logo-syllabic on the sources read, so the two notes are inconsistent. This note was not made to match the older one, because changing Linear B's classification is a claim about Linear B that should rest on a source about Linear B.
- **`wikidata` for the Unicode block.** Q30972 is the script. Q18354185 is the Unicode block U+10600-1077F, recorded here in prose because the schema has one `wikidata` field and the script is the right referent for it.
